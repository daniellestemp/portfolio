Information as a Human Right: Privacy, Democracy, and the BLS Controversy

Danielle Stemper | Pratt Institute | Fall 2025

Written for INFO 619 Information & Human Rights, this essay examines information as a human right and the ethical, legal, and governance frameworks that underpin it. The summary below is a condensed overview — the full essay, with citations, lives on the original site.

Overview

The essay opens with President Trump's August 2025 dismissal of Bureau of Labor Statistics director Erika McEntarfer following the release of weaker-than-expected jobs figures, and uses it as a case study in how political interference in statistical reporting threatens public trust in nonpartisan data institutions.

Drawing on Julie Cohen's concept of privacy as the "breathing room" democracy needs for citizens to develop independent, critical judgment, the essay argues that eroding trust in official data shrinks that room and leaves citizens more open to manipulation — what Cohen calls a "modulated democracy."

It then turns to Jonathan van Geuns and Ana Brandusescu's work on alternative data-governance models — commons, trusts, and collaboratives — as structural ways to shift power away from centralized authorities and protect information as a shared public good, including examples like Indigenous data sovereignty.

The piece closes by arguing that structural safeguards alone aren't enough: media literacy and public feedback loops, such as citizen-built dashboards, are equally necessary so people can critically interpret data releases like seasonal adjustments to employment figures, rather than accepting headline numbers at face value. Ultimately, it frames information as both an individual human right and a collective condition for democratic self-government — one that requires transparent institutions and an engaged, literate public to protect.
